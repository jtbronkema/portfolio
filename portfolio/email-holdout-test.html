Problem Statement

Every week, Fresh Thyme Market sends an email to members of its MyThyme loyalty program who have opted in to receive emails. The Digital Marketing team wanted to know whether those emails actually change customer behavior: do customers who get the email spend more and visit the store more often than customers who don’t? I partnered with the team to design a controlled experiment and answer that question with a two-sample t-test.

How We Set Up the Study

To isolate the effect of the email, we needed customers who were as similar as possible except for whether they received it. We took the full list of more than 560,000 MyThyme members who were enrolled in the program and opted in to emails, and randomly split them into two groups:

  1. Email group (80%): received the weekly MyThyme email as usual
  2. Holdout group (20%): did not receive the email

Because customers were assigned at random, the two groups had the same mix of shoppers, so any difference in sales or store visits between them can be attributed to the email itself. We then tracked sales and store visits for every customer in both groups and compared the groups with a two-sample t-test.

The Two-Sample t-Test

A two-sample t-test checks whether the averages of two separate groups are truly different, or whether the gap could have happened by random chance. We used Welch’s version of the test, which doesn’t assume the two groups have the same spread, a good fit here because the groups were different sizes.

The two-sample t-statistic (Welch’s t-test)
t = x̄1 − x̄2√s12n1 + s22n2
x̄1, x̄2Average sales per customer in the email group and the holdout group
s1, s2Standard deviation of sales per customer in each group (how spread out spending is)
n1, n2Number of customers in each group (~448,000 and ~112,000)

How it works for the email test: the top of the formula is the difference in average sales between customers who got the email and customers who didn’t. The bottom is the standard error: it combines how much spending varies within each group with how many customers are in each group, to estimate how much that difference would bounce around by chance alone. Dividing the two gives the t-statistic. The bigger it is, the less likely the difference is a fluke. A t-statistic above about 1.96 (a p-value below 0.05) means the difference is statistically significant at 95% confidence.

Worked example: sales per customer
  1. Email group: x̄1 = $18.78  (s1 = $42.10, n1 = 448,000)
  2. Holdout group: x̄2 = $18.42  (s2 = $41.60, n2 = 112,000)
  3. Difference: $18.7771 − $18.42 = $0.357 more per emailed customer
  4. Standard error: √( 42.10² / 448,000 + 41.60² / 112,000 ) = $0.1393
  5. t-statistic: 0.357 / 0.1393 = 2.56  →  p = 0.010
  6. Total impact: $0.357 × 448,000 emailed customers = $160K

We ran the same test on whether each customer visited a store, which showed how many additional shoppers the email brought in each week.

The Analysis in Python

mythyme_email_ttest.py
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

# Opted-in MyThyme members with their sales and store visits during the test
customers = pd.read_csv("mythyme_email_test.csv")

# Randomly assign 80% to receive the weekly email and 20% to the holdout
rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=42)
customers["group"] = np.where(rng.random(len(customers)) < 0.80,
                              "email", "holdout")

email = customers[customers["group"] == "email"]
holdout = customers[customers["group"] == "holdout"]

# Two-sample t-test (Welch's, unequal variances) on sales per customer
t_sales, p_sales = stats.ttest_ind(email["sales"], holdout["sales"],
                                   equal_var=False)

# Same test on whether each customer shopped in store (1 = visited)
t_visit, p_visit = stats.ttest_ind(email["visited"], holdout["visited"],
                                   equal_var=False)

# Translate per-customer lift into total impact for the emailed group
sales_lift = (email["sales"].mean() - holdout["sales"].mean()) * len(email)
visit_lift = (email["visited"].mean() - holdout["visited"].mean()) * len(email)

print(f"Sales:  t={t_sales:.2f}, p={p_sales:.4f}, lift=${sales_lift:,.0f}")
print(f"Visits: t={t_visit:.2f}, p={p_visit:.4f}, lift={visit_lift:,.0f}")
